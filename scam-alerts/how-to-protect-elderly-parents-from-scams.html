How to Protect Elderly Parents From Scams: A Guide for Adult Children

By the Safekeep team · Updated October 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: The most effective protection is a mix of conversation and simple safeguards, set up together rather than imposed. Talk openly about current scams, agree on a family code word, adopt one rule (never move money because of a call, text, or visitor), turn on bank alerts and a trusted contact, and practice spotting scams regularly. The goal is to support your parent’s independence, not take it away.

Why do aging parents need extra protection now?

Because the losses are larger and the scams are better. Adults 60 and older reported $7.75 billion in losses to the FBI in 2025, up 59% in a single year (HousingWire, summarizing the FBI IC3 report). Older adults actually report losing money less often than younger people, but when they do, they lose more: people 80 and older reported a median loss above $1,600, the highest of any age group (ABC17 News, citing FTC data).

AI has also erased many of the old tells. Scammers can clone a grandchild’s voice, write flawless emails, and fake caller ID. Being sharp is no longer enough on its own; having a plan is what works.

Step 1: How do you start the conversation without offending them?

Lead with respect and with yourself. Scams target everyone, and framing it as “we’re all being targeted” lands better than “I’m worried about you.”

  • Share a scam you or a friend received recently, and ask if they’ve seen anything similar.
  • Ask questions instead of giving rules: “What would you do if someone called saying they were from your bank?”
  • Show real examples. The simulations on the Safekeep homepage are a low-pressure way to start.
  • Make it ongoing. One talk fades; a five-minute check-in every few weeks sticks.
  • Promise no judgment. People who feel ashamed hide losses, and hidden losses can’t be stopped.

Step 2: Set up a family code word

Agree on a secret word or phrase, in person, that anyone in the family must give before asking for money in an emergency. It defeats AI voice-cloning calls, because a cloned voice can’t know a secret that was never posted online. See our full guide to the AI grandparent scam.

Step 3: Agree on three simple rules

Rules beat case-by-case judgment under pressure. Write them on a card by the phone:

  1. We never move money because of a call, text, email, or visitor. Not to a “safe account,” not into gold, not to a courier.
  2. We hang up and call back using a number we already know: the one on the card, the statement, or in the contacts list.
  3. We call family before paying anyone we didn’t contact first. Even if they say it’s secret. Especially if they say it’s secret.

Step 4: Add financial safeguards

These take an afternoon and run quietly in the background:

  • Transaction alerts. Turn on text or email alerts for large withdrawals, transfers, and new payees, and with your parent’s permission, have alerts copied to you.
  • A trusted contact. Most banks and brokerages let account holders name a trusted contact person the firm can reach if it suspects fraud or exploitation. It gives no access to the money.
  • A credit freeze. Freezing credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion is free and stops new accounts being opened in your parent’s name.
  • Legal planning. A durable power of attorney, set up with an elder-law attorney while your parent is well, makes it easier to act quickly if something goes wrong.

Step 5: Cut down the contact scammers can make

  • Turn on the phone’s spam filtering or “silence unknown callers” setting.
  • Register their numbers at donotcall.gov to reduce legitimate telemarketing, so the remaining unsolicited calls stand out.
  • Make social media profiles private and limit public videos, which are raw material for voice cloning.
  • Opt out of pre-screened credit offers at optoutprescreen.com to reduce mail that can be stolen.

Step 6: Practice regularly

Knowing about scams and recognizing one in the moment are different skills. Practice builds the second one: seeing realistic examples, making the call, and learning what gave it away.

What are the signs a parent is being scammed?

Scammers coach victims to keep secrets, so watch for behavior, not confessions. Warning signs include missed bill payments, new “friends” you’ve never met who seem to have influence, and unusual spending such as large withdrawals, checks written to cash, or frequent use of a new card (KESQ). Also watch for:

  • Secretive phone calls, or anxiety when the phone rings.
  • Purchases of gift cards, gold, or cryptocurrency, or visits to a Bitcoin ATM.
  • Remote-access software on their computer they can’t explain.
  • Talk of a prize, an investment that “can’t lose,” or an online romance.

What should you do if your parent has been scammed?

Start with reassurance: “This happens to smart, careful people, and we’ll handle it together.” Then act quickly, because money can sometimes be frozen if it’s reported fast.

  1. Stop contact with the scammer and don’t send anything more.
  2. Call the bank or card issuer using the number on the card.
  3. Report to the FBI at ic3.gov and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  4. Call the National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311), for free help with reports (U.S. DOJ).
  5. Freeze credit, change passwords, and have any remotely accessed computer checked.
  6. Warn them about recovery scams: anyone promising to get the money back for a fee is the next scam.

Frequently asked questions

How do I protect my elderly parent from scams without being controlling?

Involve them in every decision, explain the reason for each safeguard, and keep their access to their own money. Safeguards like alerts and a trusted contact add a second set of eyes without taking control away.

What is the most common scam targeting elderly parents?

Impostor scams are the ones parents encounter most: fake grandchildren, banks, tech support, and government agents. By dollars lost, fake investment schemes are the largest.

Should I monitor my parent’s bank account?

Only with their agreement. Copied alerts for large transactions are a respectful middle ground that most parents accept.

My parent refuses to believe they were scammed. What now?

Avoid arguing about the scammer. Focus on verifiable facts, such as calling the bank together on its official number, and involve a trusted third party like their banker, doctor, or attorney.

Does practice really help people avoid scams?

Practice can’t guarantee safety, but rehearsing the moment of pressure helps people pause and verify instead of reacting, which is what stops most scams.
