The AI Voice-Cloning Grandparent Scam: How to Tell If It’s Really Your Grandchild

By the Safekeep team · Updated October 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer: In the AI version of the grandparent scam, criminals copy a relative’s voice from a short clip they’ve posted online and then call you sounding frightened, asking for money for bail, a hospital, or a lawyer, and begging you not to tell anyone. Since the voice can sound exactly right, it helps to have another way to check. Ask for your family’s code word, and then hang up and call your grandchild on the number you already have for them.

What is the grandparent scam?

It’s a call or message from someone pretending to be a grandchild, a son or daughter, or another relative who’s in trouble. Usually they say they’ve been arrested, been in a car accident, or ended up in the hospital. Often a second person gets on the line, calling themselves a lawyer, a police officer, or a bail bondsman, to explain how to send the money.

Scammers keep using it because it plays on how much people love their families. In March 2025, federal prosecutors in Vermont charged 25 Canadian nationals accused of running grandparent-scam call centers near Montreal. According to the charges, callers posed as grandchildren who’d been arrested after a car accident, told victims a “gag order” meant they couldn’t tell anyone, and then sent a fake bail bondsman to the house to collect cash. Prosecutors say the group took more than $21 million from older Americans in Vermont and more than 40 other states between 2021 and 2024 (U.S. Attorney’s Office, District of Vermont).

How does AI voice cloning make it more convincing?

In the past, many people caught on because the voice didn’t sound quite like their grandson or granddaughter. That’s much less reliable now. The FTC has warned that a scammer only needs a short audio clip of a family member’s voice to imitate them (FTC), and that kind of audio is easy to find in birthday videos, social media posts, church livestreams, or a voicemail greeting.

The FBI received more than 3,100 complaints from people 60 and older that mentioned AI in 2025, and those cases added up to more than $352 million in losses (FBI IC3 2025 report, pp. 46–47). Scammers can also make caller ID show a different number, so the call might even look like it’s coming from someone you know.

What does a fake grandchild call sound like?

The calls tend to follow a familiar pattern. Even if the voice sounds just like your grandchild, it’s worth pausing if you notice some of these:

  • A sudden emergency, like being in jail, in a crash, in the hospital, or stuck somewhere far away.
  • Crying, mumbling, or a bad connection, which make it harder to notice anything off and harder to ask questions.
  • A plea not to tell their mom or dad. That request is there to stop you from making the phone call that would clear things up.
  • Someone else coming on the line who sounds calm and official, like a lawyer or an officer.
  • Pressure to pay today, often with a story that they’ll stay in jail unless bail is paid within hours.
  • Unusual ways to pay, like gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or cash that a courier will come to your house to pick up.

Some scammers take it further with what’s called a “virtual kidnapping.” You hear a loved one screaming in the background while a stranger demands a ransom and threatens them if you hang up. It’s terrifying, and it relies on the same technology. The same steps below apply.

How can you tell if a call from a family member is real?

Since you can’t rely on the sound of the voice, the best approach is to check before you do anything. It only takes a couple of minutes.

  1. Ask for your family code word. If the caller can’t give it, or changes the subject, treat the call as a scam.
  2. Hang up and call your grandchild back on the number saved in your phone, not the number that called you. If it really was them, they’ll understand why you did it.
  3. Call someone else in the family. A parent, a brother or sister, or a spouse can often tell you within minutes where the person actually is.
  4. Ask something only the real person would know, ideally something that’s never been mentioned online, like a family joke or the name of an old pet.
  5. Don’t hand money to a courier. Courts, jails, and lawyers don’t send people to your house to collect cash or gold, and the FBI has warned that grandparent scams are increasingly ending with exactly that kind of pickup, sometimes by a hired driver who has no idea what he’s part of (FBI Boston).

It helps to hear what one of these calls sounds like before it happens. Safekeep’s weekly practice includes realistic family-emergency calls and texts, followed by a short explanation of the warning signs. .

How do you set up a family code word?

The FBI suggests that families agree on a secret word or phrase that a relative has to say before anyone sends money (FBI public service announcement, December 2024). Even a convincing copy of someone’s voice won’t come with your family’s word, as long as it’s never been written down or posted anywhere.

A few tips that make it work better:

  • Pick it in person, at the next family dinner or visit.
  • Choose something easy for you to remember but hard for anyone else to guess, so not a pet’s name, a street, or anything that’s been on social media.
  • Don’t text it, email it, or post it anywhere.
  • Agree as a family that any urgent request for money has to include the word.
  • Try it once. Have a grandchild call pretending to need help, and practice asking for the word before reacting.

What should you do if you already sent money?

Act quickly. Call your bank, or the company that issued the gift card, using the number on the back of the card, and ask if the payment can be stopped. Then report it to the FBI at ic3.gov and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and call your local police if someone came to your home to collect money. The Department of Justice’s National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311), can help you with the reports. Scammers often call back for more, so it’s a good idea to let unknown numbers go to voicemail for a while.

Frequently asked questions

How much audio does a scammer need to clone a voice?

Not much. The FTC says a short clip of someone’s voice is enough, and a video posted on social media or a voicemail greeting can provide it.

Can scammers make the call look like it’s coming from my grandchild’s phone?

Yes, caller ID can be faked. That’s why it’s safer to hang up and dial the number saved in your phone yourself, instead of calling back the number that called you.

Should I stop answering calls from unknown numbers?

Letting unknown numbers go to voicemail is a sensible habit. If you do pick up, you don’t have to say much until you know who’s calling.

What if the caller says I’ll get my grandchild in trouble by calling their parents?

That’s one of the clearest signs you’re dealing with a scam. A real emergency doesn’t require keeping the rest of the family in the dark.

Do real lawyers or bail bondsmen ask for gift cards or cash pickups?

No. Courts and attorneys don’t accept gift cards or cryptocurrency, and they don’t send someone to your door to collect cash.
