This Week’s Scam Alert: Midterm Election Scams (October 19, 2026)

By the Safekeep team · Updated October 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Quick answer: Election scams use a real candidate’s name, photo, or even an AI-made voice to ask for donations, sell merchandise, or collect your personal details. The FBI warned about them on October 15, 2026. If a midterm call or text asks for money or information, skip its link and give through the campaign’s own website, and check your registration at vote.gov.

What are midterm election scams?

In the weeks before an election, phones and inboxes fill up with political messages, and most of them are real. Scammers take advantage of that by borrowing candidates’ names, logos, and slogans so their requests look like the rest. On October 15, 2026, the FBI put out a public service announcement saying it expects scammers to use the 2026 midterms this way, as they have in earlier elections, and it updated a similar warning it issued before the 2024 election (FBI IC3).

The FBI also says scammers are now using AI to make voices, pictures, and written messages that seem to come from candidates, campaigns, government offices, or people you trust (FBI IC3). So a phone call that sounds like a familiar politician, or a video of one asking for help, can be faked.

How do election scams work?

The FBI describes four versions that it’s seeing (FBI IC3):

  • A campaign “investment pool,” where you’re told that if you put money into a pool for a candidate, you’ll get a guaranteed payout after they win, and that you’ll earn a bigger share if you bring in friends and family.
  • Fake political action committees, which look like a PAC, super PAC, or campaign committee, but have nothing to do with the candidate, so the people behind them keep the money.
  • Merchandise that never arrives, where a website sells hats, signs, or shirts with a candidate’s branding, takes your card number, and ships nothing.
  • A “you’re not registered to vote” message, a text or email saying there’s a problem with your registration and sending you to a page made to look like your state’s election site, where it asks for personal details that can later be used for identity theft.

Donating to campaigns is a normal part of an election year, and plenty of people give by phone or online without any trouble. What these scams have in common is that the request reaches you first, and it either promises something a real campaign can’t offer or asks for information it doesn’t need.

What are the warning signs of an election scam?

  • Someone says your donation will earn money or be paid back. The FBI points out that a campaign donation isn’t an investment, so it won’t grow and you won’t get it back.
  • You’re asked to recruit other people to boost your share.
  • A message says you aren’t registered and includes a link to fix it.
  • The call or text feels urgent, makes you angry or worried, or says the race depends on you giving today.
  • The phone number, email address, or website doesn’t quite match the campaign or group it claims to be.
  • A “voter” form asks for your Social Security number, bank details, or a copy of your ID.

What should you do if you get one?

If you want to give, go to the candidate’s or committee’s own website by typing the address yourself, or mail a check to the address they publish. The FBI suggests looking up a PAC or committee on the Federal Election Commission’s website, where you can see whether it’s registered and how it spends its money (FBI IC3). Keep in mind that a fake committee can still be registered, so it’s worth looking at how much of its money goes to “consulting” and “administrative” costs.

If a message says something’s wrong with your registration, don’t tap the link. You can check your status yourself through vote.gov, which sends you to your state’s official site, or call your local election office. And if a voice on the phone sounds like a candidate you know, that by itself doesn’t tell you much this year, so it’s fine to hang up and look the campaign up on your own.

Practice helps with calls like these. A polished political pitch can be hard to turn down in the moment, especially when it’s about something you care about. Also this week

Two jury duty scammers were sentenced in Georgia. On October 14, a federal judge sentenced two Georgia state prison inmates to 120 months and 200 months in prison for a phone scheme in which they pretended to be local police, spoofed real police department numbers, and told people they’d missed jury duty and needed to pay a bond, mostly with gift cards (Hoodline). Prosecutors documented 119 victims and more than $464,920 in losses when the pair were convicted in January (U.S. Attorney’s Office, Middle District of Georgia). The FBI says real law enforcement won’t demand payment by phone, and it counted 6,833 jury duty and missed-court complaints with nearly $36 million in losses from January 2025 through July 2026 (FBI IC3).

An IRS investigator warned older adults about “protect your money” calls. The head of IRS Criminal Investigation’s New York field office told WHAM that scammers often say a person’s accounts have been compromised and then push them to withdraw cash, buy gold, or hand money over for safekeeping (WHAM). A real government agent won’t ask you to pull out your savings, so a request like that is a good moment to hang up. Our guide to the phantom hacker and gold bar courier scam walks through how these calls usually unfold.

Michigan issued Medicare open enrollment tips. On October 12, Michigan’s insurance and health departments reminded people that Medicare doesn’t call or visit homes, that agents can’t claim to be from Medicare, and that a Part D drug plan is voluntary, whatever a caller says (Michigan DIFS). Open enrollment runs through December 7, and our Medicare scam calls guide covers the six pitches to hang up on.

West Virginia’s U.S. Attorneys joined AARP for a fraud town hall. The two federal prosecutors spoke on an AARP West Virginia tele-town hall about romance scams, tech support fraud, and government impersonation, and their main advice was to hang up when a caller rushes you (WV MetroNews). They also noted that the IRS usually reaches people by mail first. If you’d like to talk a call through with someone, the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline is free at 877-908-3360.

What to do if it already happened

  1. Call your bank or card company using the number on the back of your card, and tell them you may have paid a scammer. The sooner you call, the better the chance of stopping a payment.
  2. Report it to the FBI at ic3.gov and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Include the phone numbers, websites, and payment details you have.
  3. If you’re 60 or older, the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311) can help you figure out where to report and what to do next.
  4. If you shared your Social Security number on a fake voter page, think about freezing your credit with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.
  5. Watch out for anyone who contacts you afterward offering to get your money back for a fee. Recovery scams often follow the first one.

Frequently asked questions

Do political campaigns call and text asking for donations?

Yes, real campaigns do, which is why the fake ones blend in. If you’d like to give, it’s safest to go to the campaign’s website yourself or mail a check, rather than paying through a link or a caller you didn’t reach out to.

Can a campaign donation earn me money?

A donation won’t earn you anything. The FBI says campaign donations aren’t investments, so they won’t grow in value and won’t be returned. Anyone offering a payout after a candidate wins is running a scam.

I got a text saying I’m not registered to vote. Is it real?

Treat it with suspicion and don’t use the link. You can check your registration yourself through vote.gov, which points you to your state’s official site, or call your county or city election office.

Can scammers fake a candidate’s voice?

The FBI says scammers are using AI to create realistic voices, images, and messages that impersonate candidates and campaigns. A familiar voice on a call isn’t proof that it’s real, so look up the campaign yourself before giving anything.

How do I check whether a PAC is legitimate?

Search for it on the Federal Election Commission’s website at fec.gov, which shows whether a committee is registered and how it spends money. A scam PAC may still be registered, so a group that spends most of its money on consultants and fees deserves a closer look.
