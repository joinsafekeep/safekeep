**Quick answer:** The phantom hacker scam is a three-step con in which fake tech support, a fake bank fraud department, and a fake government agent convince you that hackers are after your savings and that you must move the money somewhere "safe." Newer versions send a courier to your home to collect cash or gold bars. No real bank, tech company, or government agency will ever ask you to move money, buy gold, or hand anything to a courier.

## What is the phantom hacker scam?

The FBI describes it as an evolution of the classic tech support scam, built in three phases that each make the next one more believable ([FOX LA, citing the FBI](https://foxla.com/news/phantom-hacking-what-to-know-about-a-new-scam-that-fbi-officials-are-warning-people-about)). There is never a real hacker. The "threat" is invented so that you will move your own money to the criminals.

**Phase 1: The fake tech support agent.** A pop-up, text, email, or call warns that your computer or account has been compromised and gives a number to call. The "technician" asks you to install software so they can see your screen, runs a fake virus scan, and asks you to log in to your accounts to "check for unauthorized charges." This shows them which account holds the most money ([Arrowhead Credit Union](https://arrowheadcu.org/phantom-hacker-scam)).

**Phase 2: The fake bank fraud department.** A second caller, claiming to be from your bank or brokerage, confirms that a foreign hacker is in your account. They tell you to move your money to a "safe" account, often described as a government account, by wire, cryptocurrency, or cash.

**Phase 3: The fake government agent.** If you hesitate, a third person posing as an official from the FBI, the Federal Reserve, or another agency steps in, sometimes with a letter on convincing government letterhead. Their job is to make you trust the plan and keep it secret.

## Why are couriers now picking up cash and gold?

Because wire transfers can be stopped and bank tellers ask questions. Scammers increasingly tell victims to withdraw cash or buy gold and silver, then send a courier to collect it at home or a parking lot. Victims are often given a passcode to "verify" the courier, which makes the handoff feel official ([BleepingComputer, citing the FBI](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fbi-tech-support-scams-now-use-couriers-to-collect-victims-money/)).

From 2023 through May 2025, the FBI documented at least 1,737 courier pickups of cash or gold bars nationwide, with losses of more than $186.2 million ([Athol Daily News, citing FBI Boston](https://atholdailynews.com/2025/10/12/fbi-alert-sounds-alarm-on-costly-elder-fraud-scams/)). Some couriers are hired rideshare drivers who don't know they're part of a crime ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/09/22/metro/gold-bar-scam-fbi-warning/)).

## How much are these scams costing older adults?

These are among the most expensive scams there are, because they target entire savings and retirement accounts.

- Adults 60 and older reported $1.04 billion in losses to tech and customer support scams in 2025, the second-largest category for that age group ([HousingWire, summarizing the FBI IC3 report](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fbi-seniors-cybercrime-2025/)).
- Combined losses reported by older adults who lost more than $100,000 to government and business impersonation scams rose eightfold, from $55 million in 2020 to $445 million in 2024 ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-data-show-more-four-fold-increase-reports-impersonation-scammers-stealing-tens-even-hundreds)).
- Among older adults who lost $10,000 or more to these impostors in 2024, 33% paid with cryptocurrency, 20% by bank transfer, and 16% in cash ([FTC Data Spotlight](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings)).

## What are the warning signs?

- A pop-up or message that tells you to **call a phone number** to fix a security problem.
- Anyone asking to **install software** or take control of your computer.
- A "bank" or "agent" telling you to **move money to protect it**.
- Instructions to **withdraw cash, buy gold or silver,** or use a **Bitcoin ATM**.
- Being told to **lie to your bank teller** about why you're withdrawing money.
- A **courier** or "agent" coming to your home to collect anything.
- Being told the matter is **confidential** and you can't tell family.

## How do you protect yourself?

1. **Never call a number from a pop-up.** Close the browser or restart the computer. Real security warnings don't include phone numbers.
2. **Never give remote access** to someone who contacted you first.
3. **Hang up on any "bank" call about fraud,** then call the number printed on your card or statement yourself.
4. **Remember the bright line:** no government agency or legitimate business will ever ask you to move money to keep it safe, buy precious metals, or hand cash to a courier ([FBI warning via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/fbi-issues-warning-beware-devastating-195401160.html)).
5. **Tell your bank teller the truth.** Tellers are trained to spot these scams and can help stop them.
6. **Never share your home address** or agree to meet a stranger to hand over money.

## What should you do if you already moved money?

Call your bank immediately using the number on your card and ask them to stop or recall the transfer. Report to the FBI at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) and to your local FBI field office, including names, phone numbers, account numbers, and the name of any gold dealer you used. If a courier came to your home, call local police. The National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311) can walk you through every report. Then disconnect any computer that someone accessed remotely and have it checked.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will my bank ever tell me to move money to a safe account?

No. A real bank can freeze your account to protect it. It will never ask you to move money elsewhere, withdraw cash, or buy gold.

### Does the government hold money in protective accounts for citizens?

No. The FBI, the Federal Reserve, the FTC, and the Treasury do not take custody of anyone's savings to protect them.

### Why would a scammer give me a passcode for the courier?

The passcode is theater. It makes the handoff feel official and keeps you from questioning a stranger at your door.

### Is a computer pop-up with a Microsoft or Apple phone number real?

No. Microsoft and Apple do not put phone numbers in security warnings or call you about viruses.

### What if they already have remote access to my computer?

Disconnect it from the internet, call your bank from another phone, and have the computer checked by someone you trust before using it for banking again.
