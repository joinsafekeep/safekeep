**Quick answer:** The phantom hacker scam is a three-part con. A fake tech support worker, then a fake bank fraud department, and finally a fake government agent convince you that hackers are going after your savings and that you need to move the money somewhere safe. In newer versions, a courier comes to your home to pick up cash or gold bars. Real banks, tech companies, and government agencies won't ask you to move money, buy gold, or hand anything to a courier.

## What is the phantom hacker scam?

The FBI describes it as a more elaborate version of the old tech support scam, built in three stages so that each one makes the next seem more believable ([FOX LA, citing the FBI](https://foxla.com/news/phantom-hacking-what-to-know-about-a-new-scam-that-fbi-officials-are-warning-people-about)). The hacker in the story doesn't exist. The whole thing is set up so that you move your own money into the scammers' hands while believing you're protecting it.

**Stage one is a fake tech support worker.** It usually starts with a pop-up, text, email, or phone call saying your computer or one of your accounts has been compromised, along with a number to call. The person who answers asks you to install a program so they can see your screen. They run what looks like a virus scan, then ask you to log in to your bank accounts so they can check for unauthorized charges. That gives them a look at which account has the most money in it ([Arrowhead Credit Union](https://arrowheadcu.org/phantom-hacker-scam)).

**Stage two is a call from your "bank."** Someone claiming to be from your bank or brokerage's fraud department calls to confirm that a foreign hacker has gotten into your account. They say you need to move your money to a safe account, sometimes described as a government account, by wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or cash.

**Stage three is a "government agent."** If you hesitate, a third person steps in claiming to be from the FBI, the Federal Reserve, or another agency, sometimes with an official-looking letter. Their job is to reassure you that the plan is legitimate and to keep you from telling anyone.

## Why are couriers now picking up cash and gold?

Wire transfers can sometimes be stopped, and bank tellers have gotten good at asking questions, so scammers have adapted. More and more often they tell people to withdraw cash or buy gold and silver, and then send a courier to collect it at home or in a parking lot. Victims are sometimes given a passcode to confirm the courier's identity, which makes the handoff feel more official ([BleepingComputer, citing the FBI](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fbi-tech-support-scams-now-use-couriers-to-collect-victims-money/)).

Between 2023 and May 2025, the FBI documented at least 1,737 cases nationwide where a courier picked up cash or gold bars, with losses of more than $186.2 million ([Athol Daily News, citing FBI Boston](https://atholdailynews.com/2025/10/12/fbi-alert-sounds-alarm-on-costly-elder-fraud-scams/)). Some of the couriers are hired rideshare drivers who have no idea they're part of a crime ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2025/09/22/metro/gold-bar-scam-fbi-warning/)).

## How much are these scams costing older adults?

These scams tend to go after whole savings or retirement accounts, so the losses are often very large.

- People 60 and older reported $1.04 billion in losses to tech and customer support scams in 2025, making it the second-largest category for that age group ([HousingWire, summarizing the FBI IC3 report](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fbi-seniors-cybercrime-2025/)).
- Among older adults who lost more than $100,000 each to someone impersonating the government or a business, combined losses rose about eightfold, from $55 million in 2020 to $445 million in 2024 ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-data-show-more-four-fold-increase-reports-impersonation-scammers-stealing-tens-even-hundreds)).
- Of the older adults who lost $10,000 or more to these impostors in 2024, 33% paid with cryptocurrency, 20% by bank transfer, and 16% in cash ([FTC Data Spotlight](https://ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2025/08/false-alarm-real-scam-how-scammers-are-stealing-older-adults-life-savings)).

## What are the warning signs?

Any one of these is a good reason to stop and check with someone you trust:

- A pop-up or message telling you to call a phone number to fix a security problem.
- Someone asking you to install a program or let them control your computer.
- A caller from your "bank" or a "government agency" telling you to move your money to protect it.
- Instructions to withdraw cash, buy gold or silver, or put money into a Bitcoin ATM.
- Being coached on what to say to the bank teller if they ask why you're taking money out.
- A courier or "agent" coming to your house to collect something.
- Being told the matter is confidential and you shouldn't mention it to family.

## How do you protect yourself?

1. If a pop-up gives you a number to call, don't call it. Close the browser, or restart the computer if it won't close. Real security warnings from Microsoft or Apple don't come with phone numbers.
2. Don't let anyone connect to your computer if they contacted you first.
3. If someone calls saying they're from your bank's fraud department, hang up and call the number printed on your card or statement.
4. Keep in mind that no government agency or legitimate business will ask you to move your money to keep it safe, buy precious metals, or give cash to a courier ([FBI warning via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/fbi-issues-warning-beware-devastating-195401160.html)).
5. If you're withdrawing money and the teller asks why, tell them the real reason. Tellers are trained to recognize these scams and can often help stop them.
6. Don't give out your home address or agree to meet a stranger to hand over money.

## What should you do if you already moved money?

Call your bank right away, using the number on your card, and ask them to stop or recall the transfer. Report it to the FBI at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) and to your local FBI field office, and include whatever you have: names, phone numbers, account numbers, and the name of any gold dealer you used. If a courier came to your home, call your local police as well. The National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311) can help you through each report. Then unplug any computer that someone accessed remotely and have someone you trust look it over before you use it for banking again.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will my bank ever tell me to move money to a safe account?

No. If your bank thinks your account is at risk, it can freeze it. It won't ask you to move the money somewhere else, take out cash, or buy gold.

### Does the government hold money in protective accounts for citizens?

No. The FBI, the Federal Reserve, the FTC, and the Treasury don't take people's savings into custody to protect them.

### Why would a scammer give me a passcode for the courier?

It's meant to make the handoff feel official and to make you less likely to question a stranger standing at your door.

### Is a computer pop-up with a Microsoft or Apple phone number real?

No. Microsoft and Apple don't put phone numbers in their security warnings, and they don't call people about viruses.

### What if they already have remote access to my computer?

Disconnect it from the internet, call your bank from a different phone, and have the computer checked by someone you trust before you use it for banking again.
