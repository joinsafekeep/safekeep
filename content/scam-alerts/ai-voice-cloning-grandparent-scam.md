**Quick answer:** In the AI grandparent scam, criminals copy a family member's voice from a short clip posted online, then call in a panic asking for bail, hospital, or lawyer money and begging you to keep it secret. Because the voice really does sound right, the defense is not your ears. Ask for a family code word, then hang up and call your grandchild back on the number you already have.

## What is the grandparent scam?

The grandparent scam, also called a family emergency or distress scam, is a call or message from someone pretending to be a grandchild, child, or other relative in trouble. The story is usually an arrest, a car accident, or a hospital stay. A second person often comes on the line as a "lawyer," "police officer," or "bail bondsman" to explain how to pay.

It works because it targets love, not greed. Federal prosecutors have charged members of a Canadian-based ring accused of taking about $21 million from grandparents in 46 states between 2021 and 2024 using this playbook ([AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/entities/families-of-grandparents-targeted-by-canadian-fraud-ring/)).

## How does AI voice cloning make it more convincing?

It removes the one thing that used to give the scam away: a voice that didn't quite sound right. Voice-cloning software can now imitate a person from just a few seconds of recorded audio ([KRCU Public Radio](https://www.krcu.org/2026-02-11/consumer-handbook-ai-grandparent-emergency-scams)). That audio is easy to find in birthday videos, social media posts, church livestreams, and voicemail greetings.

The FBI received more than 3,100 complaints from people 60 and older that referenced AI in 2025, with losses above $352 million ([HousingWire, summarizing the FBI IC3 report](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fbi-seniors-cybercrime-2025/)). Scammers can also fake the caller ID, so the call may even appear to come from a familiar number.

## What does a fake grandchild call sound like?

Most calls follow the same script. Watch for these signs, even when the voice is perfect:

- **A sudden emergency.** Jail, a crash, a hospital, or being stranded abroad.
- **Crying or a bad connection.** Both hide small flaws in a cloned voice and discourage questions.
- **"Please don't tell Mom and Dad."** Secrecy keeps you from making the one call that would expose the lie.
- **A handoff to an "official."** A lawyer, officer, or court clerk who sounds calm and professional.
- **Pressure to pay today.** Bail must be paid within hours, or the grandchild stays in jail.
- **Strange payment instructions.** Gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or cash handed to a courier who comes to your home.

A darker variation is the "virtual kidnapping," where you hear a loved one screaming in the background while a stranger demands ransom and threatens harm if you hang up. It uses the same tools and has the same defense.

## How can you tell if a call from a family member is real?

You can't tell by listening, so verify instead. These steps take less than two minutes and work every time.

1. **Ask for the family code word.** If the caller can't give it, or dodges, treat the call as a scam.
2. **Hang up and call back.** Use the number saved in your phone, not the one that just called you. A real grandchild will understand.
3. **Call someone else in the family.** A parent, sibling, or spouse can usually confirm where the person really is within minutes.
4. **Ask a question only they would know.** Pick something that has never been posted online, such as the name of a childhood pet or a family joke.
5. **Never pay a courier.** No court, jail, or lawyer sends someone to your door to collect cash or gold. The FBI has warned that grandparent scams increasingly end with a courier pickup ([Athol Daily News, citing FBI Boston](https://atholdailynews.com/2025/10/12/fbi-alert-sounds-alarm-on-costly-elder-fraud-scams/)).

## How do you set up a family code word?

The FBI recommends a secret word or phrase that a relative must know before anyone sends money ([Money Crashers](https://www.moneycrashers.com/family-code-word-voice-clone/)). A cloned voice can copy how someone sounds, but not a secret it never heard.

- Choose it in person, at the next family dinner or visit.
- Make it memorable but impossible to guess: not a pet's name, street, or anything on social media.
- Never text, email, or post it.
- Agree that any urgent request for money or help must include it, no exceptions.
- Practice once: have a grandchild call and ask for help, and ask for the word before reacting.

## What should you do if you already sent money?

Move quickly. Call your bank or the gift card company using the number on the back of the card and ask them to stop the payment. Report the scam to the FBI at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) and the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/), and call your local police if a courier came to your home. The Department of Justice's National Elder Fraud Hotline, 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311), can help you file. Expect the scammers to call back for more; don't answer unknown numbers for a while.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much audio does a scammer need to clone a voice?

Only a few seconds of clear speech, according to consumer protection guidance. A short video posted on social media or a voicemail greeting can be enough.

### Can scammers make the call look like it's from my grandchild's phone?

Yes. Caller ID can be faked, which is why you should hang up and dial the number saved in your phone yourself rather than calling back.

### Should I stop answering calls from unknown numbers?

Letting unknown numbers go to voicemail is a reasonable habit. If you do answer, avoid saying much until you know who is calling.

### What if the caller says I'll get my grandchild in trouble by calling their parents?

That instruction is the clearest sign of a scam. A real emergency never requires secrecy from the rest of the family.

### Do real lawyers or bail bondsmen ask for gift cards or cash pickups?

No. Legitimate courts and attorneys do not accept gift cards, cryptocurrency, or cash handed to a courier at your door.
