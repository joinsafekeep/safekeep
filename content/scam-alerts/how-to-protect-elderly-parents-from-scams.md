**Quick answer:** What tends to work best is a combination of open conversation and a few simple safeguards that you set up together with your parent. Talk about the scams going around, agree on a family code word, settle on a rule that nobody moves money because of a call, text, or visitor, turn on bank alerts and name a trusted contact, and look at example scams together now and then. Done this way, your parent stays in charge of their own money and has some backup.

## Why do aging parents need extra protection now?

Losses among older adults have climbed steeply, and the scams themselves have gotten more convincing. People 60 and older reported $7.75 billion in losses to the FBI in 2025, up 59% in a single year ([HousingWire, summarizing the FBI IC3 report](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fbi-seniors-cybercrime-2025/)). Interestingly, older adults report losing money less often than younger people do, but when they lose money they tend to lose more. People 80 and older reported a median loss above $1,600, the highest of any age group ([ABC17 News, citing FTC data](https://abc17news.com/stacker-financial-protection/2026/10/12/the-scams-targeting-you-most-by-age-group/)).

AI has also made some of the old warning signs less reliable. Scammers can now copy a grandchild's voice, write emails with perfect grammar, and make caller ID show a familiar number. Even very sharp people get fooled, so it helps to have a plan worked out in advance.

## How do you start the conversation without offending them?

Most parents don't want to be treated as though they can't handle things, and nobody does. It usually goes better if you talk about scams as something everyone is dealing with, yourself included, instead of starting with "I'm worried about you."

- Mention a scam text or call you got recently and ask whether they've seen anything like it.
- Ask what they'd do in a situation, like getting a call from someone saying they're from their bank, instead of handing them a list of rules.
- Look at real examples together. The example simulations on the [Safekeep homepage](/#examples) can be an easy way to start.
- Come back to it every few weeks for a few minutes. A single big talk is easy to forget.
- Make it clear you won't judge them if something ever happens. People who feel ashamed tend to hide losses, and a loss that's hidden is much harder to stop.

## Should your family have a code word?

It's one of the simplest things you can do. Agree in person on a secret word or phrase that anyone in the family has to say before asking for money in an emergency. Scammers can now copy someone's voice from a short video online. A word your family has never written down or posted is something they'd have no way to find out. Our article on the [AI grandparent scam](/scam-alerts/ai-voice-cloning-grandparent-scam) goes into more detail.

## What rules should the family agree on?

In the moment, with a frightened voice on the phone, it's hard for anyone to weigh things carefully. A few rules agreed on in advance take some of that pressure off. Some families write them on a card and keep it by the phone:

1. We don't move money because of a phone call, text, email, or someone at the door. That includes moving it to a "safe account," buying gold, or handing it to a courier.
2. If something seems urgent, we hang up and call back using a number we already know, like the one on the back of the card, on a statement, or saved in our contacts.
3. Before paying anyone we didn't contact first, we call someone in the family. That goes double if the caller has asked us to keep it a secret.

## What financial safeguards help?

These take an afternoon to set up and mostly run in the background afterward:

- **Transaction alerts.** Most banks can send a text or email for large withdrawals, transfers, or new payees. With your parent's permission, you can have copies sent to you too.
- **A trusted contact.** Most banks and brokerages let account holders name a trusted contact person the firm can call if it suspects fraud or exploitation. Naming someone doesn't give them any access to the money.
- **A credit freeze.** Freezing credit at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion is free, and it stops anyone from opening new accounts in your parent's name.
- **Legal planning.** A durable power of attorney, set up with an elder-law attorney while your parent is healthy, makes it much easier to act quickly if something does go wrong.

## How can you cut down on scam calls and messages?

- Turn on spam filtering on their phone, or the setting that sends unknown callers straight to voicemail.
- Register their numbers at donotcall.gov. It won't stop scammers, but it cuts down on legitimate telemarketing, which makes the suspicious calls easier to notice.
- Set social media profiles to private and think twice before posting videos where their voice is clear, since those can be used to clone a voice.
- Opt out of pre-approved credit offers at optoutprescreen.com, so there's less mail worth stealing.

## What are the signs a parent is being scammed?

Scammers often coach people to keep quiet, so you're more likely to notice a change in behavior than to hear about it directly. Common signs include missed bill payments, new "friends" you've never met who seem to have a lot of influence, and unusual spending, like large withdrawals, checks written to cash, or heavy use of a new credit card ([KESQ](https://kesq.com/news/2026/10/18/how-to-prevent-elder-fraud-and-financial-abuse/)). You might also notice:

- Secretive phone calls, or nervousness when the phone rings.
- Gift card, gold, or cryptocurrency purchases, or trips to a Bitcoin ATM.
- Remote-access software on their computer that they can't explain.
- Talk about a prize, an investment that "can't lose," or someone special they met online.

## What should you do if your parent has been scammed?

Start by reassuring them. Something like, "This happens to smart, careful people all the time, and we'll sort it out together," can make it easier for them to tell you everything. Then move quickly, because money can sometimes be frozen if it's reported soon enough.

1. Stop all contact with the scammer and don't send anything else.
2. Call the bank or card company, using the number on the card.
3. Report it to the FBI at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) and to the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).
4. Call the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311) for free help with the reports ([U.S. DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/usao-or/pr/department-justice-charges-unprecedented-number-elder-fraud-defendants-nationwide-and)).
5. Freeze their credit, change passwords, and have any computer that a scammer accessed checked out.
6. Let them know that anyone who calls offering to get the money back for a fee is almost certainly running another scam.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I protect my elderly parent from scams without being controlling?

Involve them in each decision, explain why you're suggesting it, and make sure they keep full access to their own money. Alerts and a trusted contact give you a way to spot problems early without taking anything out of their hands.

### What is the most common scam targeting elderly parents?

The calls and messages parents get most often come from impostors pretending to be a grandchild, a bank, tech support, or a government agency. By dollars lost, fake investment schemes are the biggest problem.

### Should I monitor my parent's bank account?

Only if they agree to it. Having alerts for large transactions copied to you is a respectful middle ground that many parents are comfortable with.

### My parent refuses to believe they were scammed. What now?

Arguing about the scammer usually doesn't help. It tends to work better to focus on things you can check together, like calling the bank on its official number, or to bring in someone they trust, like their banker, doctor, or lawyer.

### Does practice really help people avoid scams?

Nothing can guarantee someone won't be fooled. Practicing what to do when the pressure is on does make it more likely that a person will stop and check before acting, and that's often enough to stop a scam.
